All of the drum machine. None of the hardware.

The best of all worlds.

The immediacy of a drum machine and the depth of a DAW, in one window. STEPPER has every basic you need to make a beat: pads, sequencer, piano roll, mixer, effects, plugins, audio tracks, song arrangement and export. Nothing you need is missing, and nothing you don't is in the way.

Sixteen pads. Zero excuses.

Keyboard, MIDI or mouse. Every pad has trim, chops, reverse, stretch, pitch, volume and pan, one shot or gate, choke and a channel of its own. This is the real pad surface, drawn by STEPPER itself. Go on, hit one.

On a Mac, the keys 5 6 7 8, R T Z U, F G H J and C V B N play them too.

Stepping it up.

The sequencer and the piano roll edit the same notes. The grid on the left covers every pad. The roll on the right gives one pad its melody. Tap a step, tap a note, press play. Pick a pad by tapping its name.

Fifteen effects. All the right side effects.

On a pad, a channel or the master, in any order you like. These are STEPPER's own effect windows, with its own drawing code and its own knobs. Turn them, switch them with the dot, and press play to hear the beat through them.

Plays well with others.

Audio Units and VST3, right in the chain, beside the built-in effects and in the export too. Every plugin is tested in a separate process when STEPPER scans for it. If one crashes, STEPPER keeps running and simply doesn't offer it.

It's what's on the outside, too.

The whole program changes its material. The 3D skins are drawn as a lit machine, from camouflage and brushed steel to clear plastic. Add the flat 2D skins and you get 21. Tap one to see it up top.

Phone it in. Literally.

No app to install. STEPPER serves the page to your iPhone itself, in the same skin. Skin, page, names and lights follow the program live. Below is that very page. Upright, it plays the pads. On its side, it plays the piano roll.

USB-C or Wi-Fi

By cable through Personal Hotspot, or on the same Wi-Fi. Add it to your Home Screen once and you're set.

Cool under pressure.

The interface, the network and the pictures all stay out of the audio's way. In a 60-second load test at full tilt, with a 128-sample buffer, it didn't drop out once.
